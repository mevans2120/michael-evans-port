## The Story (Flowing Narrative)

### Breaking Up with Amazon

When e-commerce first became mainstream, Target made what seemed pragmatic: partner with Amazon to sell everything online. Amazon had the infrastructure, the expertise, the platform.

Target quickly realized this wasn't sustainable. They were giving Amazon valuable customer data and relationships. Limited in their ability to control the experience. Building Amazon's business more than their own. Unable to differentiate their digital experience.

Target initiated a massive project to move from Amazon to their own proprietary e-commerce engine. This wasn't just a platform migration—it was a fundamental shift in how Target approached digital commerce.

Target was one of Huge's biggest clients, generating the most revenue that year. The scale was massive—I traveled to Minneapolis every week, working on approximately 20 different concurrent projects. My role encompassed both tactical and strategic work: project manager for individual work streams, program manager coordinating across all Target projects, strategic partner helping define the future of retail.

### Strategy That Inspires

One of the biggest learnings from the Target engagement was **the value of having a strategy that inspires people**.

**Target's strategy:** Being the place that people love and selling products people love.

**Walmart's strategy** (by contrast): More transactional, efficiency-focused, price-driven.

This difference in strategic positioning affected everything—how we designed experiences, what we optimized for (love vs. efficiency), how we talked about the work, how Target team members engaged with customers.

The partnership worked so well because Target's inspirational strategy gave us a North Star. We weren't just building an e-commerce platform—we were creating a place people would love to shop. That's a fundamentally different design challenge.

### Empower, Don't Replace

Beyond the immediate e-commerce work, we did forward-thinking conceptual work on the future of retail. Looking back from 2025, it's fascinating to see what came to pass.

**The big strategic insight:**

**What DOESN'T work:** Putting kiosks inside retail stores. People don't really love using kiosks—they're often grimy, confusing, and rarely better than talking to an employee or shopping on your own phone.

**What DOES work:** Building tools to make team members smarter. Give employees technology to know more about inventory (what's in stock, where it is), understand how inventory flows through the store, access customer information to provide better service, have as much information as possible to be gracious, helpful team members.

**The philosophy:** Technology should empower employees, not replace them. The future of retail isn't eliminating human interaction—it's making human interactions more informed, more helpful, and more delightful.

This became one of the bigger strategic decisions that you've seen come to light across retail. Modern retail increasingly focuses on empowering store employees with mobile tools rather than forcing customers to use in-store kiosks.

### Data Modernization

Beyond design and strategy, I led significant work modernizing how Target thought about data. Target had real data science issues with how they reported their numbers. Traditional retail metrics didn't translate well to e-commerce.

We created dashboards that showed meaningful conversion metrics, drop-off points in the purchase funnel, return customer behavior and patterns. A much more modern way to think about their business with data.

At enterprise scale, data infrastructure and analytics are as important as the customer-facing experience. You can't optimize what you can't measure, and you can't measure what you don't instrument properly.

### Managing at Scale

Managing 20+ concurrent work streams for an enterprise client taught invaluable lessons about program management and strategic coordination.

**The challenges:** Multiple teams working on interdependent features. Different timelines and priorities. Need for consistent design language and UX patterns. Technical dependencies between work streams. Weekly travel to Minneapolis for in-person meetings. Aligning Huge's design team with Sapient Nitro's development team. Managing Target's internal stakeholders across departments.

**What worked:** Clear strategy gave us a decision-making framework. Regular cadence with weekly in-person meetings maintained alignment. Distributed ownership with individual project managers for each work stream. Program-level oversight coordinating across all projects. Partnership mindset—treating Target as a partner, not just a client.

### Reflections

**Scale requires different approaches.** Managing 20 projects isn't 20 times harder than managing one—it's exponentially harder. You need different tools, processes, and mindsets at enterprise scale.

**Strategy alignment is everything.** With multiple teams and stakeholders, a clear, inspirational strategy is the difference between chaos and coordination.

**Long-term partnerships beat transactional projects.** The weekly travel, the ongoing relationship, the deep understanding of Target's business—these created value that short-term projects never could.

**Predictions are hard.** I was wrong about QR codes. Being humble about what the future holds and staying curious about emerging technologies is essential.

**The bigger lesson:** Technology should make shopping more delightful, employees more capable, and businesses more sustainable—in that order. Not replace employees with kiosks. Not prioritize efficiency over experience. Not copy what tech companies do without understanding retail.
